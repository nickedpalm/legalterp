A paralegal I know spent three days trying to book a Mandarin interpreter for a federal immigration hearing, only to discover that the interpreter she'd hired — credentialed, professional-looking website, glowing reviews — wasn't on any court roster. The case got delayed. The client stayed detained longer. And the attorney had to explain to a federal judge why they hadn't verified certification status before showing up.

Nobody warns you how granular these requirements get.

> **The Short Version:** Court interpreter certification is not one thing — it's a patchwork of federal law, state-level exam systems, and language-specific credential tiers. For any legal proceeding, the interpreter's credential status must match the specific court, language, and proceeding type. "Professional interpreter" ≠ "court-certified interpreter."

## Key Takeaways

- The 1978 Court Interpreters Act (28 USC §1827) created a federal mandate, but certification is administered separately at the federal and state levels
- Federal court certification currently exists for only three languages: Spanish, Navajo, and Haitian Creole
- State court exams have two credential tiers — "Certified" (oral exam required) and "Registered" (proficiency-only) — and which tier applies depends entirely on what language you're working in
- Maintenance requirements are ongoing: [Washington](/locations/district-of-columbia/washington-dc/) requires 16 CE credits and 20 courtroom hours every two years just to stay active

## The Federal Layer: Narrower Than You Think

The Court Interpreters Act of 1978 — codified at 28 USC §1827 — established that federal proceedings must provide interpreters to limited English proficient (LEP) defendants and witnesses. Civil Rights Act Title VI (1964) and Executive Order 13166 (2000) extended that obligation across federally funded programs.

Here's what most guides skip: the federal *certification exam* currently covers only three languages. Spanish. Navajo. Haitian Creole.

That's it. The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts has administered the Federal Court Interpreter Certification Examination (FCICE) since 1980, but full oral certification testing exists for Spanish only on a regular basis. For every other language in federal court, interpreters operate under a different qualification framework — one that doesn't carry the same certification designation.

> **Reality Check:** If someone tells you they're "federally certified" in Cantonese or Vietnamese, ask them to clarify. Federal certification for those languages doesn't exist. They may be highly qualified, but the specific credential they're describing doesn't exist under the FCICE framework.

The next written FCICE exam registration opens April 8, 2026 — worth flagging if you're advising anyone pursuing this path.

## State Court Certification: Two Tiers, Not One

The National Center for State Courts (NCSC) developed a shared exam system that multiple states use, which at least brings some consistency. But the two-tier structure trips people up constantly.

**Certified status** requires passing a full oral exam testing three court interpretation modes: simultaneous, consecutive, and sight translation. Washington State benchmarks are representative of the national standard: written exam ≥80%, then oral exam ≥70% in *each* of the three sections. Miss one section and you don't pass — partial scores don't carry over.

**Registered status** exists for languages where no oral exam has been developed. It requires demonstrated language proficiency and the same background check, ethics training, and oath — but no court-mode oral exam. This matters because the credential shown on a court roster reads differently, and some courts treat these tiers differently for high-stakes proceedings.

The list of languages where full certification is even possible varies by state:

| State | Certified Languages Available | Credential Tiers | CE Maintenance |
|-------|-------------------------------|------------------|----------------|
| Washington | 13 (Arabic, Bosnian/Croatian/Serbian, Cantonese, French, Khmer, Korean, Laotian, Mandarin, Portuguese, Russian, Spanish, Tagalog, Vietnamese) | Certified + Registered | 16 CE credits + 20 courtroom hours / 2 years |
| California | 12 (Arabic, Cantonese, Eastern Armenian, Farsi, Filipino/Tagalog, Korean, Mandarin, Portuguese, Punjabi, Russian, Spanish, Vietnamese) | Certified + Registered | State-mandated CE |
| Federal | 3 (Spanish, Navajo, Haitian Creole) | FCICE Certified only | N/A (exam-based) |

California also recognizes ASL court interpreters through reciprocity with Texas BEI or RID Legal Specialist credentials — a workaround that's become a model other states are watching.

## What the Process Actually Looks Like

Washington's credentialing process is a useful benchmark because it's among the most documented. The AOC publishes the full sequence:

1. Register for and pass the written exam (English vocabulary, court terminology, ethics) — ≥80%
2. Complete fingerprint background check and submit certification letter
3. Attend a court interpreter orientation
4. Complete an ethics course
5. For certified languages: pass the oral exam — ≥70% per section (simultaneous, consecutive, sight translation)
6. Take the oath and receive ID badge + roster placement

The whole process takes more than a year in Washington — that's not an estimate, it's the official language from the AOC. Plan accordingly.

> **Pro Tip:** The written exam is the first eliminator, not the last. Free prep through programs like Interpretrain exists specifically for this stage. Don't skip it on the assumption that your bilingual fluency will carry you — the written exam tests court-specific terminology and ethics scenarios that most fluent speakers haven't studied.

## Prerequisites Nobody Reads Carefully

The baseline requirements are consistent enough across jurisdictions to treat as universal:

- At least 18 years old
- U.S. work authorization
- High school diploma or equivalent
- High proficiency in English *and* the target language (tested separately)

That last point catches people. Bilingual fluency is necessary but not sufficient. Courts test court-specific register — the ability to render formal legal language into colloquial equivalents without altering substance, and vice versa. Your job isn't translation in the literary sense; it's precise, mode-accurate rendering under pressure. The oral exam tests exactly that.

> **Important:** Interpreters must preserve register. Rendering a judge's formal instruction as informal language, or cleaning up a witness's fragmented colloquial answer into clean prose, are both ethics violations. The rules don't allow you to "help" either party by editing what was said.

## The Admissibility Problem

Here's where this gets high-stakes for attorneys: interpreted testimony from an uncredentialed or improperly credentialed interpreter can be challenged on admissibility grounds. Courts in states with official roster systems — like Washington and California — rely on those rosters as the verification mechanism. If the interpreter your client hired isn't on the roster for that court's jurisdiction, you may have a problem that surfaces at the worst possible moment.

Check the interpreter's credential against the court's specific roster. Not just "are they certified somewhere" — are they on *this* court's approved list, for *this* language, at *this* credential tier.

That's the detail that cost the paralegal's client three extra days in detention.

## Practical Bottom Line

If you're hiring a court interpreter:

1. **Ask for their credential tier** — Certified or Registered, and in which language
2. **Verify on the official court roster** — state AOC databases and the federal court's interpreter management system are public
3. **Match the credential to the proceeding** — immigration courts, state criminal, and federal civil have different requirements
4. **For federal proceedings**, confirm Spanish FCICE certification or ask what their specific qualification is for other languages

If you're pursuing certification:

1. Start the written exam prep now — free resources exist, the process takes 12+ months
2. Federal written registration opens April 8, 2026 for Spanish
3. Check your state's AOC for which languages have oral exams available — this determines your credential ceiling
