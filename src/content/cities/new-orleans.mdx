## How to Choose a Certified Court Interpreter in New Orleans

- **Match certification to venue.** Federal proceedings in the Eastern District of Louisiana require FCICE-certified interpreters. Louisiana state court proceedings are governed by NCSC standards. Immigration matters before the NOLA EOIR immigration court require DOJ EOIR accreditation. Using the wrong credential tier has gotten interpreted testimony excluded — don't assume one cert covers all venues.
- **Verify the language pair specifically.** "Spanish interpreter" is not a credential. Ask for their language pair as tested — many interpreters hold certification in one direction only (e.g., Spanish→English) and are less reliable in the reverse. For Vietnamese and French Creole assignments, ask for documentation of any language-pair-specific testing, since FCICE currently only tests Spanish.
- **Ask about courtroom mode — simultaneous vs. consecutive.** Depositions typically run consecutive. Trial work often requires simultaneous with equipment. Make sure the interpreter has logged hours in the mode you need, not just a credential that doesn't distinguish.
- **Check NAJIT membership and disciplinary history.** NAJIT's directory and ethics complaint process are public. A working professional in New Orleans should be able to point you to their NAJIT profile, ATA listing, or state court roster.
- **Confirm availability with turnaround time in mind.** For multi-day trials, lock the interpreter down before you lock the hearing date. Last-minute replacements in specialized language pairs (Haitian Creole, Mandarin, Arabic) can blow up a scheduling order.

> **Pro Tip:** Louisiana's Code of Evidence Article 404 and court interpreter oath requirements are stricter than most attorneys realize. Ask your interpreter directly: "Have you testified about your qualifications before a Louisiana judge?" If they hesitate, that's data.

## What to Expect

Rates in New Orleans typically run **$350–$750 per assignment**, with half-day and full-day minimums common for court appearances. Depositions often bill at an hourly rate with a two-hour floor; trial days bill flat. Turnaround for scheduling a credentialed interpreter in major language pairs (Spanish, Vietnamese) is typically 48–72 hours; less common language pairs need a week or more of lead time.

> **Reality Check:** The cheapest interpreter in the room is rarely a deal. In Louisiana, if interpreted testimony is later challenged on competency grounds, you're looking at a possible retrial — not a refund. The $150 you saved on interpreter fees is not worth the exposure. Credential verification takes five minutes. Do it.

New Orleans sits at the intersection of Gulf Coast immigration corridors and a dense federal court docket — the Eastern District is one of the busier maritime and criminal federal courts in the South, and the NOLA immigration court handles a steady volume of asylum and removal proceedings driven by Central American and Vietnamese communities that have anchored in the metro since the 1970s. That volume means qualified interpreters are working, not sitting idle — which is exactly why early booking and credential confirmation aren't optional here.
