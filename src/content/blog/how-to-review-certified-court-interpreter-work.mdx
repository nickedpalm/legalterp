The first time I sat across from a certified interpreter who wasn't — and I only figured it out halfway through a three-hour deposition — was the day I started building this checklist.

The interpreter had a credential. It said "Registered" on her profile. She spoke both languages fluently. But somewhere in hour two, I noticed the attorney kept pausing and asking the witness to repeat answers. The witness looked confused. The interpreter was softening testimony. Not lying, exactly. Paraphrasing. Editorializing. Stripping out the anger in the witness's voice and handing the attorney something bland and safe.

Nobody had given us a framework for catching that in real time.

> **The Short Version:** Reviewing a court interpreter's work means checking five things: certification tier, linguistic accuracy, ethical neutrality, mode proficiency, and platform competence. The FCICE allows a 20% error margin as the outer acceptable limit — anything beyond that is grounds for re-work. A downloadable-style checklist follows.

---

**Key Takeaways**
- "Certified" and "Registered" are not the same tier — only criterion-referenced exam holders meet federal and most state standards
- Accuracy isn't just about words; tone, register, and intent all count as fidelity failures when missing
- Ethics violations (softening, editorializing, siding) are harder to catch than vocabulary errors but matter more
- Virtual proceedings add a fifth dimension — platform competence is now a legitimate evaluation category

## The Certification Problem Nobody Talks About

Here's what most people miss: "certified" is a marketing word before it's a legal standard. The National Center for State Courts (NCSC) tracks at least six distinct credential tiers across state programs — Certified, Registered, Master, Qualified, Provisional, and Approved. Some of those tiers require criterion-referenced examinations. Some do not.

The Federal Court Interpreter Certification Examination (FCICE) is a criterion-referenced standard. So are most state "Certified" designations through NCSC-aligned programs. "Provisional" and "Approved" tiers typically mean the court couldn't find a certified interpreter and is working with someone who passed a less rigorous screen.

The difference matters because FCICE sets a **20% error margin as the maximum acceptable under quality standards** — meaning even the pass threshold assumes imperfect performance, calibrated for real-world pressure conditions. Someone who cleared a provisional screen hasn't had their work measured against that standard at all.

> **Reality Check:** If your interpreter's credential isn't FCICE, a state Certified designation, or NAJIT-accredited, you're working with an interpreter whose accuracy has never been formally benchmarked. That's not automatically disqualifying — but it means *you* become the quality check.

## The Quality Checklist

Use this during or after any proceeding where accuracy is case-consequential. Two categories: what you can verify before the assignment, and what you evaluate during and after.

### Pre-Assignment Verification

| Item | Standard | Pass/Fail |
|---|---|---|
| Credential tier | FCICE, state Certified, or NAJIT-accredited | ☐ |
| Language pair documented | Source and target language explicitly listed | ☐ |
| Legal specialization confirmed | Courtroom experience, not general or medical | ☐ |
| Mode proficiency declared | Simultaneous, consecutive, and sight translation | ☐ |
| Platform competence (if remote) | Zoom/Teams tested prior to proceeding | ☐ |
| Ethics code on file | NAJIT Canon or court-specific equivalent | ☐ |

### During and Post-Proceeding Review

| Item | What You're Checking | Pass/Fail |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy and fidelity | No omissions, additions, or meaning drift | ☐ |
| Tone preservation | Anger, hesitation, sarcasm conveyed — not flattened | ☐ |
| Register matching | Formal language in formal exchanges, plain when plain | ☐ |
| Terminology consistency | Same legal term used consistently throughout | ☐ |
| Clarification requests | Interpreter pauses correctly when unclear rather than guessing | ☐ |
| Neutrality | No editorial commentary, no coaching, no softening | ☐ |
| Pace and flow | Consecutive mode doesn't significantly compress or expand | ☐ |
| Legal citations | Statute references, case names repeated verbatim | ☐ |
| Cultural competence | Idioms and culturally-specific references handled accurately | ☐ |
| Composure under pressure | No visible distress, delivery stays smooth during contentious exchanges | ☐ |

## Fidelity Is About More Than Words

I'll be honest — when I first started thinking about interpreter quality, I focused almost entirely on vocabulary. Does she know what *voir dire* means? Can he translate *hearsay* without losing the legal weight?

That's table stakes. The harder thing to evaluate is fidelity of *intent*.

If a witness says something angry, the interpreter must convey that anger. If a witness hedges — "I *believe* I may have seen" — the interpreter can't clean that up into "I saw." If an attorney uses deliberate ambiguity, the interpretation can't resolve it. The Flores (2005) study on healthcare interpreters identified domain-specific vocabulary as the leading error category, but in legal settings, tone and intent failures often cause more damage because they affect the jury's read of the witness.

> **Pro Tip:** If you have a bilingual colleague available, ask them to monitor a portion of the proceeding. You're not looking for perfection — you're looking for patterns. Consistent register flattening or softened emotional content signals a fidelity problem worth flagging before testimony becomes part of the record.

## When to Request Re-Work

Three situations warrant formal re-work or replacement:

**1. Accuracy failures above the 20% threshold.** The FCICE's quality standard gives us a benchmark: if errors exceed one in five interpreted segments, performance has dropped below federally recognized minimums. In practice, if you or a reviewer catch more than two or three clear omissions or distortions in a 15-minute block, flag it.

**2. Neutrality violations.** An interpreter who edits testimony, coaches a witness's answer, or expresses visible sympathy is compromising the record. This isn't a performance issue — it's an ethics issue. Document it, stop the proceeding if you can, and request a replacement.

**3. Consistent terminology drift.** If the same legal term is being rendered differently across the proceeding — "objection" as "complaint" in one exchange and "protest" in another — the record becomes unreliable. Consistent terminology is one of the clearest, most auditable standards you can apply.

> **Reality Check:** Dramatically lower interpreter rates are a red flag, not a deal. Certified interpreters command premium rates because the credential is genuinely hard to obtain. If you're getting a price that seems too good, ask about the certification tier before you book.

## The Virtual Proceeding Wrinkle

Remote hearings on Zoom and Teams added a legitimate fifth evaluation dimension that didn't exist ten years ago. Platform issues — dropped audio, lag between channels, poor microphone positioning — can degrade even an excellent interpreter's output to the point where the record is compromised.

Test platform competence before the proceeding. Confirm the interpreter has used the specific platform in a legal context. Verify microphone quality and audio isolation. Position matters here too — interpreters doing simultaneous mode need a separate audio channel from the main proceeding feed, not just the same call.

## Practical Bottom Line

You can't outsource quality control on interpreted testimony. The checklist above gives you a structured framework, but the core habit is simple: treat the interpreter's output as a deliverable with a standard, not a service you take on faith.

Before the proceeding: verify credential tier, confirm mode proficiency, test the platform.

During: monitor tone and register, not just word choice.

After: check for consistency, flag neutrality issues immediately, and document anything that crossed the 20% error threshold.

The interpreters who clear this checklist easily are the ones worth booking again. The ones who bristle at the question of credentials are usually the ones whose credentials wouldn't survive the question.
